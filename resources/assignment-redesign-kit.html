Issue 002 · Teacher resource

The assignment redesign kit

A ten-minute planning sheet, a worked example and student-facing instructions. Start with one assignment.

Read the articleDownload infographic

Type into the planning sheet, then print or save as PDF using your browser. Entries stay on this page only and disappear when you reload; nothing is submitted. The printout includes your answers and the worked example below.

Part 1 · Your ten-minute redesign

Name the thinking, not just the finished product: evaluate, interpret, justify, debug, compare…

Choose a five-minute classroom checkpoint that shows a starting claim, method or interpretation. Keep appropriate supports.

Use a short note: “I changed ___ because ___. I rejected ___ because ___.”

Change a condition, remove a source or ask students to apply a principle to a fresh example. Choose an accessible in-class response mode.

State allowed AI use, prohibited use, acknowledgement, a route without AI and one existing requirement you will remove or shorten.

Part 2 · A worked redesign

Original assignment: Write a 700-word argument about whether a community should reduce single-use plastics. Use three sources. Submit Friday.

Learning target: judge source quality, use evidence to justify a recommendation and address a counterargument. This example assesses evidence-based reasoning. If original prose is the target, revise the AI permissions accordingly.

Before class: select three short, age-appropriate sources with different perspectives. Provide complete citations and accessible copies; check facts and readability. This kit does not supply a plastics research pack. You can instead use sources already attached to an assignment in your subject.

Feedback criteria

  • Accurate evidence: describes a relevant source fairly and recognises a limitation.
  • Justified choices: links a recommendation or revision to evidence and considers an alternative.
  • Independent explanation: gives a defensible response to the changed condition with agreed supports.

Use these alongside your subject rubric. Do not grade accent, confidence or willingness to use AI. A hesitant response is not evidence of misconduct. Follow your school’s procedures where concerns remain.

A workload trade

Replace an existing exit ticket with the explanation. Scan the checkpoint to plan feedback; do not give every checkpoint a lengthy written comment. Schedule any necessary individual follow-ups across lessons. Adjust timing for your class and accommodations.

Part 3 · Student-facing instructions

Adapt the model permissions below to your school policy, approved tools and assessment target before assigning.

Your task: Write a 700-word argument recommending whether a community should reduce single-use plastics. Use the three supplied sources, acknowledge a counterargument and explain your reasoning.

Start in class: Before you use AI, write a provisional claim, one supporting passage with a source reference, and one question or limitation. You have five minutes, with the sources and your agreed accessibility supports.

AI boundaries: After the checkpoint, you may use a school-approved AI tool to suggest counterarguments or feedback questions. Check every claim against the supplied sources. AI must not write your argument, checkpoint, decision note or final in-class explanation, or invent citations. You may use peer feedback instead; AI use earns no extra credit.

With your argument: Add about 100 words: “I changed ___ because ___. The evidence that mattered was ___. I rejected ___ because ___.” Then state “No AI used” or name the permitted tool and task, and describe one suggestion you accepted, changed or rejected. Do not include private information or a full chat history.

Explain in class: Respond to a new question that changes a condition in the task. You may use your source pack and agreed supports, but not generative AI. Your teacher will arrange an equivalent response mode where needed.

What counts: accurate evidence, justified choices and an explanation that shows your understanding, together with the subject rubric.

Part 4 · Make it work in your subject

  • Science: explain how a changed control variable would affect a conclusion.
  • English: defend an interpretation using a passage not discussed in the submitted response.
  • Mathematics: explain why a method fits, then handle a changed constraint.
  • Programming: predict a function’s behaviour on a new input, then explain a small fix.
  • Arts / technology: explain a design trade-off and revise it for a changed audience or constraint.

Choose an explanation that actually matches the learning target. A retrospective note or recording alone does not guarantee independent work. Combine evidence over time and preserve agreed accommodations.
