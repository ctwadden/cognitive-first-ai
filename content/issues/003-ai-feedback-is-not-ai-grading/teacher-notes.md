# Teacher notes: Comment, Confirm, Decide

## Preparation

Print one copy of the student activity for each student or display it and have students answer on paper. No AI account is needed. Allow about 12 minutes: two minutes to read, four minutes for Steps 1–3, four minutes for Steps 4–5, and two minutes for the exit question and a quick scan of responses.

The routine has three moves. The prepared AI-style comment is the **Comment**. Steps 3–5 ask students to **Confirm** their thinking without AI. The evidence you see or hear supports your **Decision** about whether to reteach, prompt, extend or move on.

## Worked answers

1. A defensible choice is “Add one sentence explaining why shade lowers temperature.” It gives the writer an action. A strong response may add that the advice is only partly useful: explaining a mechanism does not fix the weak causal claim or missing comparison data. A weak response underlines “Strong answer” without explaining how this helps the writer improve.

2. Students might circle “clearly showed that the canopies were effective” or the prepared response's claim that the canopies “caused” an exact 5°C reduction. The two measurements differ by 5°C, but different days and unrecorded conditions mean the source cannot isolate the canopy as the cause. A weak response says all the feedback is wrong simply because it is AI-style feedback.

3. A strong completion distinguishes the visible product from the learner. For example: “The feedback can tell us that the response uses both temperatures, but it cannot tell us whether the writer understands why the comparison is insufficient to prove cause.” Accept other clear distinctions, such as whether the writer can transfer the idea to a fresh example. A weak response says only that AI “does not know anything.”

4. A strong revision makes a cautious claim and names a limitation. For example: “The recorded surface temperature was 5°C lower after the canopies were installed. This is consistent with the canopies helping, but the two readings alone do not prove that the canopies caused the difference because weather conditions and unshaded bus stops were not compared.” A weak response keeps the causal claim and merely adds that shade blocks sunlight.

5. Strong answers propose relevant comparison evidence and explain its value. Examples include recording temperatures at canopy and no-canopy stops at the same times, repeating readings across many comparable days, or recording cloud cover, air temperature and wind. These controls or repeated measures help separate a canopy effect from weather or day-to-day variation. A weak response asks only for “more information” without naming what or why.

For the exit question, either choice can be defended. The revision shows whether the student can improve the original claim. The fresh question is usually stronger evidence of transfer because the student must identify a new way to test the claim without copying the feedback. Look for the quality of the explanation, not the selected option.

## Common misconceptions

1. **“A 5°C difference proves a 5°C effect.”** Prompt: “What else changed between the two measurements, and what stayed comparable?”

2. **“If one sentence of feedback is useful, the whole judgement must be reliable.”** Prompt: “Can an actionable suggestion sit beside an unsupported conclusion? Mark each part separately.”

3. **“A correct revision proves the student can transfer the reasoning.”** Prompt: “What fresh question would require the same idea without repeating the same words?”

## Adaptations

For students who need more support, provide these sentence frames: “The data show ___, but they do not prove ___ because ___” and “Riverton should also collect ___ so that ___.” Read the fictional source aloud and ask students to highlight what was measured and what was not.

For extension, ask students to design a fair four-stop comparison plan. They should specify when measurements occur, what conditions are recorded and what result would make them more or less confident in a canopy effect.

Where writing is a barrier, let the student give a 30-second oral response or draw a comparison diagram with two branches: canopy stops and no-canopy stops. Ask them to label what must be kept similar.

## What this shows you

Steps 1–3 show whether students can separate an actionable comment from an unsupported judgement. Step 4 shows whether they can revise a claim to match evidence. Step 5 and the exit question provide stronger evidence of independent reasoning and transfer.

The activity does not produce a grade. You decide which response is sufficient evidence for the learning intention, whether follow-up is needed, and whether any assessment decision is warranted.

This routine is a proposed classroom application, not a tested intervention.
