# Teacher notes and worked answers
Try → Check → Explain • Suggested use: 10 minutes

## Preparation — about 2 minutes
Print or display the fictional data first. Keep the deliberately flawed answer and these teacher notes hidden until the appropriate step. Explain that the values were invented for this task. No student data needs to be collected or entered into an AI tool.

The learning target is evidence-based explanation: describe an association, identify an unsupported causal claim and explain a limitation. Suitable for mathematics, science, social studies or a literacy lesson on evaluating claims. Check that students have encountered the idea of a fair comparison; introduce it briefly if needed.

## Worked answer: TRY
“In this fictional table, the groups reporting more recreational screen time have lower average quiz scores; Group A’s average is 12 percentage points higher than Group C’s.”

This describes the supplied pattern without claiming that it must hold outside this table. Six percentage points is the difference between adjacent group averages. It is not a predicted improvement for any individual.

Useful questions include: How many people are in each group? How were the groups chosen? How was screen time measured? Were the quizzes comparable? Did the groups have similar prior knowledge, study time or sleep?

## Worked answer: CHECK
Overclaims include “proves,” “causes,” “every student,” “will rise” and “exactly.”

One possible alternative explanation is that study time differs between the groups. Sleep, prior knowledge or differences in the quizzes might also matter. These are possibilities, not facts established by the table.

A careful rewrite: “The fictional groups with more screen time have lower average scores; Group A averages 78% and Group B 72%. The table does not establish whether screen time caused that difference or predict what would happen to one student who changed their habits.”

Do not teach the opposite overclaim: the table does not prove that screen time has NO effect either. It leaves the causal question unresolved.

## Worked answer: EXPLAIN
“The rows compare different groups, so the score differences might reflect factors besides screen time. I would investigate whether the groups had similar prior knowledge and study time before predicting an effect for an individual.”

Accept other valid explanations. Listen for three elements: a correct pattern, a reason the causal inference is too strong, and a relevant next question. Use this as a formative observation, not a score assigned by AI.

## Support and extension
Support: read the sample aloud; provide the sentence starter “The table shows ___, but it does not tell us ___”; accept a recorded or live oral explanation through your usual approved classroom process. Keep private student recordings out of AI prompts.

Extension: ask students to outline a fairer way to investigate the causal question and identify a practical or ethical limitation. They need not propose an experiment on actual classmates.

## Optional teacher-only prompt for a future example
“Create a short, clearly fictional dataset for my [subject and level] lesson on [reasoning skill]. Label it invented. Write one deliberately overconfident interpretation, then a teacher-only explanation of its flaw and a careful correction. Do not use real people or personal information. Keep the dataset internally consistent. I will check it before classroom use.”

A prompt is a request, not a guarantee. Check all values, calculations and explanations before use. Student AI use is not required.
